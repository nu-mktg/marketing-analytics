## PART 1: Concepts and Mathematics

---

### Section 1.1 — Math Toolkit

---

#### Tool 1: Geometric Series Sum

When you multiply the same number by itself repeatedly, the sum can be calculated compactly.

Throughout this lecture **λ (lambda) is the decay parameter** — the fraction of last week's advertising that is still working this week. Section 1.4A defines it properly; that one sentence is all you need to follow the arithmetic here.

If λ = 0.7 and you spent $100 in week 1 only, the carryover in weeks 2, 3, 4 is:
> Week 2: 0.7 × 100 = 70
>
> Week 3: 0.7 × 70 = 49
>
> Week 4: 0.7 × 49 = 34.3

Add up what that one week's \$100 is still worth, week after week. **Count week 1 itself**, because the
\$100 works in the week you spend it too:

> 100 + 70 + 49 + 34.3 + ... = **333.3**

⚠️ **Mind what is being added.** The 233.3 in weeks 2, 3, 4, … is the *carryover*; the 333.3 is the
**total**, carryover plus the original \$100. Both numbers are used in this lecture and they are 100
apart, so say which one you mean.

**Where the formula comes from** — one line, because you will use it twice. Each week is λ times the
week before, so the total is

> 100 + 100λ + 100λ² + 100λ³ + ... = 100 × (1 + λ + λ² + ...)

Call that bracket $T$. Multiply it by λ and every term shifts down one: $λT = λ + λ² + λ³ + ...$,
which is $T$ with its leading 1 removed. So $T - λT = 1$, giving $T = 1/(1-λ)$ — and the total is
100 × 1/(1 − 0.7) = **333.3**.

**Formula:** Sum = Initial / (1 − λ), valid when 0 ≤ λ < 1. (The restriction is what makes the sum
settle: at λ ≥ 1 each week is at least as large as the last and the total grows without limit.)

⚠️ **This is a sum *over time*, generated by one week's spend.** The identical expression turns up again in Section 1.4A as a **level** — the adstock you are carrying in a typical week when you spend the same amount *every* week. Same algebra, two different questions, and Part B of the homework uses the second one. Section 1.4A derives it and says why one formula answers both.

---

#### Tool 2: Fraction Arithmetic

The Hill function produces numbers between 0 and 1. You need to compare fractions. Its general form, defined in Section 1.4B, is

> H(s) = s^α / (EC50^α + s^α)

where **EC50** is the spend level that puts the channel at half its maximum effect and **α** is the exponent — the shape parameter. Every worked example in this lecture, and Part A of the homework, **chooses α = 2**, so the numbers you compute by hand come from the same function with the exponent filled in:

> H(s) = s² / (EC50² + s²)

Those are not two Hill functions. They are one function written twice, once in general and once at the α these examples choose. α = 2 is the examples' choice, not a fact about advertising: Part B of the homework does not assume α at all — it **fits** α from the data, and the fitted value need not be 2. (Section 1.4B also pins down *which* spend s is: the channel's **adstocked** spend, not the raw spend of that one week.)

When s = EC50: H(EC50) = EC50² / (EC50² + EC50²) = EC50² / (2 × EC50²) = **1/2 = 0.5**

This is true regardless of what EC50 equals — the numerator and denominator are always equal at s = EC50. It is also true regardless of α, for the same reason (Section 1.4B).

---

#### Tool 3: The Slope of a Curve — "What Does the Next Dollar Buy?"

A **slope** answers exactly one question: if I add one more unit of input, how much more output do I get?

For a straight line the answer is the same everywhere. For a **curved** line it is not — the slope belongs to the *point you are standing at*, not to the whole curve. The Hill function is curved, so "what does the next dollar buy?" has a different answer at every spend level. That answer is called the **Hill slope**, written H′(s): the slope of H at the point s.

Two ways to get it, and this lecture uses both. Take EC50 = 200 and α = 2, the Part 1 Checkpoint's curve:

- **Average slope over a stretch** — the change in H divided by the change in s. From s = 100 to s = 200, H goes 0.20 → 0.50, so the average slope is (0.50 − 0.20) / 100 = **0.0030 per unit**. From 200 to 400, H goes 0.50 → 0.80, so (0.80 − 0.50) / 200 = **0.0015** — half as much per unit of spend. That fall *is* diminishing returns, measured.
- **Exact slope at a point** — the calculus version. Section 1.4C derives it as a formula, using one tool (the chain rule) that it then uses twice more; on this curve it reads H′(100) = 0.0032, H′(200) = 0.0025, H′(400) = 0.0008.

The slope does not fall everywhere, and Section 1.3 explains why: below the inflection at ≈ 115 it is still *rising* (H′(50) = 0.0022, **less** than H′(100) = 0.0032). It peaks at the inflection and falls from there on.

⚠️ **H and H′ are different numbers answering different questions, and the lecture needs both.** H(s) = 0.50 says *how much of the channel's ceiling you have already collected*. H′(s) = 0.0025 says *how much more the next unit collects*. A channel that is mostly saturated has a high H and a low H′. Budget decisions run on the second one — Section 1.4C builds marginal ROI out of it.

### Section 1.2 — Business Motivation

---

#### The Attribution Problem

If a customer buys after clicking a paid search ad, last-touch attribution gives 100% of the credit to paid search. But they may have first learned about your brand through a TV spot, then seen a social post three times before finally clicking the paid search ad.

Jonathan Becker, who has managed over $3.5 billion in paid acquisition:

> *"Paid search captures demand. It does not create it."*

A campaign that shifts budget from TV to paid search may look successful in the short term — paid search conversions appear to increase. But you are harvesting awareness that TV built. Over time, organic awareness declines and paid search efficiency falls.

Marketing Mix Modeling is the analytical response to this problem: it models all channels simultaneously, accounting for their different timing and saturation dynamics.

### Section 1.3 — Conceptual Framework

---

#### The Two Core Nonlinearities

MMM handles two effects that make simple regression inadequate:

**1. Carryover (Adstock):** Advertising effects do not disappear instantly. A TV spot seen on Monday influences purchase decisions through the week. The adstock transformation models this decay explicitly.

**2. Diminishing Returns (Saturation):** The first $10,000 spent on a channel has more impact than the $100,001st dollar. Doubling spend does not double revenue. The Hill function models this **saturating** relationship — a curve that rises toward a ceiling it never passes.

⚠️ **"Saturating" is not the same as "concave everywhere," and a Hill curve need not be concave everywhere.** The Hill function has a shape parameter α (Section 1.4B). It is concave from the very first dollar only when **α ≤ 1**. With **α > 1** — including α = 2, the value every worked example in this lecture chooses — the curve is **S-shaped**: returns *increase* over an initial stretch, then turn over and diminish. The turning point (the inflection) sits at

$$s^* = EC_{50}\left(\frac{\alpha - 1}{\alpha + 1}\right)^{1/\alpha}, \qquad \text{which for } \alpha = 2 \text{ is } \frac{EC_{50}}{\sqrt{3}} \approx 0.577 \times EC_{50}.$$

(A Deep Dive in Section 1.4C derives this formula; here it is enough to know where the turn is.)

So with EC50 = 200 and α = 2, the first 100 buys +0.20 of effectiveness and the *second* 100 buys **+0.30** — more, not less. Diminishing returns only take over above ≈115. The Part 1 Checkpoint's own numbers (H(100) = 0.20, H(200) = 0.50, H(400) = 0.80) show exactly this, and it is why the budget rule in Section 1.4C is "shift incrementally and re-check the marginal return" rather than "returns always fall, so overshooting is safe."

**Why an S-shape is the realistic story, not a nuisance:** at very low spend a channel has not reached enough people often enough to register — a handful of impressions builds no awareness — so the first dollars genuinely under-deliver, the middle of the range is where reach and frequency compound, and only then does the audience run out. The concave-everywhere picture is the special case α ≤ 1.

**How S-shaped a real channel is, is an empirical question.** The inflection moves toward zero as α falls toward 1: at α = 1.2 it sits at about 0.14 × EC50, so over the range of spend a channel actually operates in, the curve behaves as if it were concave. Whether a channel's α is 2, 1.2 or below 1 is something the data decide — which is why Part B of the homework **fits** α instead of assuming it.

That is the condition under which no *small* move of budget between them raises total revenue. Section 1.4C explains why, with S-shaped curves, it is necessary but not by itself enough.

### Section 1.4 — Mathematical Framework

---

#### Part A: Adstock — Modeling Carryover

The adstock value at time t is:

$$A_t = S_t + \lambda \cdot A_{t-1}$$

where S_t is spend in period t and λ (lambda) is the decay parameter (0 ≤ λ < 1 — λ = 0 is allowed, and means no carryover at all; see Checkpoint Q4).

**Plain English:** This week's *effective spend* equals what you spent this week, plus a fraction λ of last week's effective spend that is still carrying over. Higher λ = slower decay = longer-lasting effects.

**What kind of number A_t is.** Unroll the recursion and A_t is a **decaying stock** of all past spend: this week's at full weight, last week's at weight λ, the week before at λ², and so on —

$$A_t = S_t + \lambda S_{t-1} + \lambda^2 S_{t-2} + \cdots$$

So A_t is **not cumulative spend** (old spend fades out instead of piling up) and **not this week's spend** (past weeks still count). It settles at a constant only in one special case — spending the same amount every week — which is the steady state at the end of this Part.

🚨 **"Effect" means two different things in MMM, and this is the line where they meet.** A_t is **adstocked spend**: it is denominated in dollars of *spend*, not dollars of revenue. When the worked example below reports A₃ = 86, it means "week 3 behaves like a week in which you had spent \$86k", **not** "\$86k of revenue". The **revenue** effect of a channel is a different quantity built in Sections 1.4B and 1.4C: β · H(A_t), in dollars of revenue. Where this lecture says "advertising effect" without qualifying it, it means A_t on the spend scale; "revenue contribution" always means β · H. Both senses are called "effect" in practice, so when you read an MMM write-up, check which one is meant.

**Worked example** (λ = 0.6, S = [100, 0, 50]):
- A₁ = 100 + 0.6 × 0 = **100**
- A₂ = 0 + 0.6 × 100 = **60** ← no spend, but carryover remains
- A₃ = 50 + 0.6 × 60 = **86**

**Comparing channels:**
- λ_TV = 0.82 → TV carryover lasts many weeks after a campaign ends
- λ_Paid_Search = 0.19 → Paid search carryover dissipates quickly (makes sense: captures in-the-moment intent)

**The same expression, two different questions.** Section 1.1's Tool 1 used 1/(1 − λ) as a **sum over time**: one week's \$100 of spend, and the carryover it generates across all the following weeks totals 100/(1 − 0.7) = 333.3. The budget-optimization step in Part B of your homework uses the identical expression as a **level**: if you spend \$100 *every* week, the adstock you are carrying settles at A = 100/(1 − 0.7) = 333.3 — the same number, but it is the value of A_t in one typical week, not a total accumulated across weeks.

One line shows why one formula answers both. Once adstock stops changing, A_t = A_{t−1} = A, so the recursion becomes

$$A = S + \lambda A \;\Longrightarrow\; A(1-\lambda) = S \;\Longrightarrow\; A = \frac{S}{1-\lambda}$$

This **steady-state** sense is the one used in Section 1.4B's "adstock ≥ raw spend" argument and in Part B of the homework, where the optimizer holds weekly spend fixed and needs the adstock level that spend sustains.

> ### 🔍 Deep Dive: Why Geometric Decay?
> Adstock uses geometric decay for mathematical tractability. The total long-run *adstock* generated by a single-period spend S is S × 1/(1−λ) — a spend-scale quantity, per the convention above, not revenue. This means a channel with λ = 0.8 generates 5× the immediate spend in long-run effect (1/(1−0.8) = 5). Geometric decay also makes the adstock recursion computationally trivial — it requires only the previous period's value, not the full history.

#### Part B: Hill Function — Modeling Saturation

The Hill function maps a spend level to effectiveness on a 0-to-1 scale:

$$H(s) = \frac{s^\alpha}{EC_{50}^\alpha + s^\alpha}$$

where **EC50** is the spend level at which the channel is at 50% of its maximum effectiveness, and
**α** is the **exponent** applied to both s and EC50 — the shape parameter. It is a plain number with
no units; it does **not** move EC50 (H(EC50) = 0.5 for every α); and what it controls is how *sharply*
the curve turns from flat to steep as spend passes EC50 — the larger α is, the more of the channel's
response is packed into a narrow band of spend around EC50. α ≤ 1 gives a concave curve; α > 1 gives
an S-shaped one that inflects at $EC_{50}((\alpha-1)/(\alpha+1))^{1/\alpha}$ (Section 1.3; derived in a Part C Deep Dive). The
worked examples in this lecture choose α = 2 — the form Section 1.1's Tool 2 wrote out; a fitted
model estimates α from the data (Part C).

**What the y-axis means.** H is the **share of the channel's maximum possible revenue contribution
that it is delivering right now.** Part C multiplies it by β_j, the most revenue channel j could ever
produce, so contribution ÷ ceiling = β_j·H ÷ β_j = H: H = 0.3 reads "this channel is producing 30% of
the most it ever could."

🚨 **Which "spend level"? Say it once, here: the argument $s$ is always the channel's ADSTOCK $A_t$,
never the raw spend $S_t$ of one week.** In Part C below, and in every model you or the agent will fit, the
Hill function is applied to $H(A_t)$ — the output of Section 1.4A's recursion, not $S_t$. So EC50
lives on the **adstock scale** too: it is the *adstocked* spend level at which the channel is half
saturated.

**Convention for the rest of this course:** wherever "spend level" appears next to EC50, a Hill
response or a saturation claim, it means **adstocked spend** $A_t$ — Part A's decaying stock, which is
neither cumulative spend nor this week's spend. This is worth stating explicitly
rather than leaving to inference, because the two scales differ by a lot and in a fixed direction:
since $A_t = S_t + \lambda A_{t-1}$ and $\lambda \geq 0$, **adstock is always at least as large as
raw spend**, and in the constant-spend special case (the steady state) $A = S/(1-\lambda)$ — four times
raw spend at λ = 0.75. Read an
EC50 of 51.5 against raw spend and a channel can look starved; read it against adstock and the same
channel can be well past half-saturation. (Section 2.2's "EC50 values" paragraph is this same
statement applied to a fitted model, not a second, different rule.)

**Key property:** H(EC50) = 0.5 always. This is because at s = EC50, the numerator and denominator are equal (EC50^α / 2×EC50^α = 0.5).

**Interpretation** — all three read on the adstock scale, per the convention above:
- H < 0.5: current adstocked spend is below EC50 — the channel is on the early part of its curve
- H = 0.5: at EC50 — the channel is at half its maximum effect
- H > 0.5: above EC50 — past half-saturation; the marginal return is falling

⚠️ **H > 0.5 means adstock is above EC50 — and says nothing about this week's spend $S_t$.** So you
*can* answer "above or below EC50?" from H alone; just read the answer as a statement about $A_t$.
Because adstock ≥ spend, "H = 0.82, so we are well above EC50" can be true while this week's $S_t$,
on its own, sits below EC50.

⚠️ **H ranks a channel against its own EC50. It does not rank channels against each other, and it is
not the budget rule.** It is tempting to read "Digital H = 0.30, TV H = 0.75" as "so move budget to
Digital". That does not follow, and Section 1.4C replaces it: what the next dollar earns in a channel
is **β** — how much revenue that channel can produce at all — times the **Hill slope** at its current
adstock (Section 1.1, Tool 3), times 1/(1 − λ) to convert from adstock to the dollars you actually
spend. A large β can outweigh sitting further up the saturation curve. H is one factor of three,
never the whole rule.

> ### 🔍 Deep Dive: Why H(EC50) = 0.5 Regardless of α
> Substituting s = EC50: H(EC50) = EC50^α / (EC50^α + EC50^α) = EC50^α / (2 × EC50^α) = 1/2. The α terms cancel completely. This is a mathematical identity, not an approximation.

#### Part C: The MMM Regression

Once we compute adstock and apply the Hill function, we fit:

$$\text{Revenue}_t = \beta_0 + \beta_{TV} H(A_{TV,t}) + \beta_{Dig} H(A_{Dig,t}) + \beta_{Soc} H(A_{Soc,t}) + \varepsilon_t$$

The **channel** coefficients — β_TV, β_Dig, β_Soc — capture the maximum revenue each channel can generate (the plateau), because H can approach 1 but never passes it. The Hill functions scale that maximum by current efficiency. **β₀ is not one of them:** it is the intercept — the revenue the model predicts when every channel's Hill response is 0, i.e. the **average level** of revenue coming from everything that is not advertising (brand equity, distribution, the product's usual price). ε_t is the regression error, as in Lecture 2: the week-to-week movement in revenue the model does not explain.

**Why brand equity belongs in β₀ but a price cut or the holiday season does not.** β₀ is a single number, so it can only hold non-ad drivers that are roughly *constant* across weeks. A driver that *moves* week to week — a promotion, the season — cannot live in a constant; in this equation it lands in ε_t. That is harmless only if it moves independently of ad spend, and it usually does not: firms advertise more in the holiday season, when revenue would have been high anyway. Then spend and ε_t are correlated, the fit credits the channel with revenue the season produced, and β is biased upward — Lecture 2's endogeneity problem, an omitted variable correlated with a regressor. That is why production MMMs add **control variables** (price, promotions, seasonality, trend) as extra regressors, so each driver is estimated instead of left in the error. The homework's model has none because its dataset was generated without such drivers; a real dataset will not be so kind.

**How the model is estimated.** The β's enter the equation linearly, so for *given* curves they are an ordinary least-squares fit — Lecture 2's regression. λ, EC50 and α do not: they sit inside the adstock and Hill transformations, so no single regression can find them. Part B of the homework therefore nests two searches. For any candidate set of the nine curve parameters (λ, EC50 and α for each of three channels), compute every channel's adstock and H, fit β₀ and the three β's by least squares, and record the total sum of squared revenue errors. An outer search — `scipy.optimize.differential_evolution` — tries candidate sets within bounds and keeps the one with the smallest error, fitting all channels **jointly** (the prompt explains why: channel by channel leaves EC50 unidentified). So λ, EC50 and α are **estimated** — the values under which the model fits revenue best — not chosen by the analyst, and the inflection needs no separate step: once α and EC50 are estimated, s* follows from the formula derived below. This is a least-squares fit, **not a Bayesian one**: it returns one best set of parameters, not a distribution over them. Production tools differ on exactly this point — Google's Meridian and PyMC-Marketing are Bayesian (Meridian samples its posterior by MCMC), while Meta's Robyn fits a regularised (ridge) regression and searches its transformation parameters with the evolutionary optimizer Nevergrad. That is context; this course does not ask you to run them.

**Marginal ROI — the number the budget decision actually runs on.** Marginal ROI is **the extra revenue that the next dollar of spend buys in a channel, evaluated at that channel's current adstock.** It is the one quantity that is comparable across channels, and it is the thing the Overview promised you would be able to interpret.

**Where it comes from: one derivative, three links.** Hold each channel's spend at the same amount
S_j every week — the steady state of Section 1.4A, which is exactly the setting Part B's optimizer
works in. Then channel j's weekly revenue contribution is β_j · H(A_j) with A_j = S_j/(1 − λ_j), and
marginal ROI is the derivative of that contribution with respect to **weekly spend S_j** — not with
respect to H, and not with respect to adstock. Revenue depends on H, H on adstock, adstock on spend,
so the **chain rule** multiplies three links:

$$\text{MR}_j \;=\; \frac{d\,\text{Rev}}{dS_j} \;=\; \underbrace{\beta_j}_{d\text{Rev}/dH} \;\times\; \underbrace{H'(A_j)}_{dH/dA} \;\times\; \underbrace{\frac{dA_j}{dS_j}}_{dA/dS}$$

The first link is immediate: revenue is β_j times H. The other two need a line each.

**Link 2 — deriving the Hill slope H′.** Write k = EC50^α to save ink. Adding and subtracting k in the
numerator, H(s) = s^α/(k + s^α) = 1 − k/(k + s^α), and only the second term moves with s. Call
u = s^α: the derivative of −k/(k + u) with respect to u is k/(k + u)², and du/ds = α s^(α−1) — the
chain rule again. Multiplying,

$$H'(s) \;=\; \frac{\alpha\, k\, s^{\alpha-1}}{\left(k + s^{\alpha}\right)^{2}} \;=\; \frac{\alpha \cdot EC_{50}^{\alpha} \cdot s^{\alpha-1}}{\left(EC_{50}^{\alpha} + s^{\alpha}\right)^{2}}, \qquad s = A_{j,t}$$

Check it against Tool 3: at EC50 = 200, α = 2, s = 100 it gives 2 × 40,000 × 100 / 50,000² = **0.0032**.

> ### 🔍 Deep Dive: Where the Inflection Formula Comes From
> The inflection is where H′ stops rising and starts falling — the peak of the bottom panel in
> Section 1.3's figure — so set the derivative of H′ to zero. It is easiest on the logarithm, which
> peaks where H′ does: ln H′ = constant + (α − 1) ln s − 2 ln(k + s^α), with derivative
> (α − 1)/s − 2α s^(α−1)/(k + s^α). Setting that to zero and multiplying through by s(k + s^α) gives
> (α − 1)(k + s^α) = 2α s^α, i.e. (α − 1)k = (α + 1)s^α, so
>
> $$s^* \;=\; EC_{50}\left(\frac{\alpha-1}{\alpha+1}\right)^{1/\alpha}$$
>
> — Section 1.3's formula. It only exists for α > 1: at α ≤ 1 the bracket is zero or negative, there
> is no peak, and H′ falls from the first dollar.

🚨 **Link 3 — you do not buy adstock. You buy SPEND, and the two are not one-for-one.** H′ is a slope
*per unit of adstock*, while marginal ROI is defined *per dollar of weekly spend*. Differentiate the
steady state A = S/(1 − λ): raising weekly spend by \$1 **every week** raises the adstock level by
exactly **1/(1 − λ)**, not 1. The same number answers the one-off question too — a single extra \$1
this week adds 1, λ, λ², … to adstock over the following weeks, a total of 1/(1 − λ) (Tool 1's sum) —
but with a condition the steady-state version does not need: those extra units land in *different*
weeks, so each is worth β · H′ only if H′ is about the same in every week the carryover lasts. That
holds when adstock stays near one level; it holds less well for a bursty schedule or a long-memory
channel. Multiplying the three links:

$$\text{MR}_j \;=\; \underbrace{\beta_j \cdot H'(A_j)}_{\text{revenue per unit of adstock}} \;\times\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{1-\lambda_j}}_{\substack{\text{adstock per dollar} \\ \text{of weekly spend}}} \;=\; \frac{\beta_j \cdot H'(A_j)}{1-\lambda_j}$$

**That last factor is not a rounding detail, because λ differs enormously across channels.** At the
lecture's own values — λ_TV = 0.82, λ_Paid_Search = 0.19 — a TV dollar buys 1/0.18 ≈ 5.6 units of
adstock and a paid-search dollar buys 1/0.81 ≈ 1.2. Rank two channels on β · H′ alone and you have
compared them on a scale neither of them buys in; the channel with the longer memory is
systematically under-credited — by 2-to-1 in the example below. This is also why Part B's agent prompt tells the optimizer to use A = S/(1 − λ): it
works in spend, which is the thing you actually control.

> ### 🔍 Worked example: what dropping 1/(1 − λ) costs
> Two channels identical in every respect except memory: β = 100, EC50 = 100 and α = 2 for both;
> λ₁ = 0.8 (long memory) and λ₂ = 0.2 (short). The total weekly budget is 100, held fixed.
>
> - **Wrong rule — equalize β · H′(A) only.** The channels share one curve, so equal slopes means
>   equal adstock: S₁/(1 − 0.8) = S₂/(1 − 0.2), i.e. S₂ = 4 S₁, so the split is **20 / 80**. Both
>   adstocks are then 100 = EC50, so H = 0.5 each and revenue = 100 × 0.5 + 100 × 0.5 = **100.0**.
> - **Right rule — the full MR.** The revenue-maximizing split, found numerically, is **40.7 / 59.3**:
>   adstocks 203.3 and 74.2, both marginal ROIs 0.772, revenue **116.0**.
> - **The cost.** The wrong rule gives the long-memory channel about half its best budget (20 against
>   40.7 — the 2-to-1) and gives up (116.0 − 100.0)/116.0 = **13.8%** of achievable revenue. At its
>   20 / 80 split β · H′ is equal, yet the true MRs are 0.5/0.2 = **2.50** and 0.5/0.8 = **0.625**: the
>   long-memory channel is returning four times as much per dollar and being starved of it.

**Three factors matter and none alone is the answer.** β_j says how much revenue the channel could
ever produce; H′ says how hard the next unit of adstock is working right now; 1/(1 − λ_j) says how
much adstock a dollar buys in the first place. A big-β channel deep into saturation can be worth
*less* at the margin than a small-β channel still early on its curve — which is why the rule below
ranks on MR and not on any single one of them — not β, not EC50, not H, and not λ.

**Budget optimization — and why revenue, not profit.** The homework holds the total budget fixed.
Then cost is the same under every allocation, so profit = margin × revenue − budget is maximized by
the allocation that maximizes revenue: **at a fixed budget, maximizing revenue *is* maximizing
profit** (given one margin across the revenue the channels drive).¹

> ¹ *If the total budget can change, a different rule applies: keep adding spend to a channel while
> margin × MR_j > 1 — that is, while MR_j > 1/margin — because the next dollar must bring back at
> least a dollar of margin. At a 40% margin a channel needs MR above \$2.50. Not used in this course.*

**The rule.** Compute marginal ROI — the full β_j · H′(A_j)/(1 − λ_j), not just the first two factors
— for each channel at its current adstock. Move one small step of budget from the lowest-MR channel
to the highest-MR channel, recompute every MR, and repeat. At the best allocation, every channel you
fund shares one MR and any channel you have dropped has an MR no higher. The reason is the
arithmetic of one step, a few paragraphs below: while two funded channels' MRs differ, a small step
from the lower to the higher gains roughly their difference, so you are not yet done.

⚠️ **Equal MR is necessary for the best interior allocation — not sufficient.** With S-shaped curves
the equal-MR condition can hold at several allocations, and some of them are among the *worst*. In
the worked example above, three splits have equal MRs: 40.7 / 59.3 (revenue 116.0, the optimum), but
also about 1 / 99 (revenue 60.7) and about 97 / 3 (revenue 96.1). Those two are revenue *minima*:
one channel is starved below its inflection, where its MR is still low but rising. Cut the budget to
50 and it gets starker — the only equal-MR split is a minimum, and the best allocation is a
**corner**: all 50 to the long-memory channel (revenue 86.2), the other dropped. So compare the
candidates' predicted revenue, corners included, instead of stopping at the first split where the
MRs match. That is what Part B's optimizer does: it searches the allocations for the highest
predicted revenue directly, rather than solving "MRs equal".

**Reporting the result — and say what the percentage is a percentage OF.** The headline number from
a reallocation is the **revenue uplift**: predicted revenue under the optimal allocation minus
predicted revenue under the current allocation, expressed as a **percentage of the current
prediction**.

$$\text{uplift}\ \% \;=\; \frac{\widehat{\text{Rev}}_{\text{optimal}} - \widehat{\text{Rev}}_{\text{current}}}{\widehat{\text{Rev}}_{\text{current}}} \times 100$$

Both predictions are at the **same total budget** — reallocation, not extra spend (Misconception 4).
Dividing by the *optimal* prediction instead, or reporting a percentage-**point** difference, gives a
different number; Part B of the homework asks for this one.

**The arithmetic of one step.** If channel A earns MR_A = \$4.00 of revenue per dollar and channel B earns MR_B = \$1.50, then moving \$1,000 from B to A gains roughly

$$\Delta\text{Revenue} \;\approx\; (\text{MR}_A - \text{MR}_B) \times \Delta\text{Spend} \;=\; (4.00 - 1.50) \times 1{,}000 \;=\; \$2{,}500$$

⚠️ **Read the ≈.** That calculation holds both marginal ROIs *fixed* across the whole \$1,000, and they are not fixed: as A gains adstock its Hill slope changes, and as B loses adstock so does B's. Which way the error runs depends on where the two channels sit. **Past both inflections** — where funded channels normally end up — A's MR falls as it gains and B's rises as it loses, so the true gain is **smaller** than \$2,500. **Below the inflections** the slopes move the other way, and the true gain can be **larger**. And over a big enough shift the two MRs cross, after which every further dollar moved *loses* revenue — so a large move can hand back much of what its first part gained. (In the worked example above, moving 5 from the 20 / 80 split gains 7.8 against a first-order 9.4; moving 60 gains nothing at all, against a first-order 112.5.) That is why the step has to be **small** — small enough that neither MR changes much across it, which is the one condition under which the first-order number can be trusted — and why the rule says "one step at a time, then re-compute". The \$1,000 here is a round illustrative step, not a recommended size; what counts as small depends on the curves. And it is why the answer to "can I move the whole budget at once?" is no. Part A of the homework asks you for this arithmetic; Part B's optimizer does the honest version, re-evaluating the curves at every candidate allocation.

### Checkpoint Answer Key

**Q1.** A₁ = 200 + 0 = 200. A₂ = 0 + 0.6×200 = 120. A₃ = 50 + 0.6×120 = 50 + 72 = **122**.
*Common wrong answer:* A₂ = 0 (forgetting carryover when no spend occurs). The recursion carries forward λ × previous adstock regardless of current spend.

**Q2.** **Above EC50.** H = 0.73 > 0.5, and H > 0.5 if and only if current **adstock** > EC50 — per Section 1.4B's convention, the "spend" in the question is adstocked spend, and H says nothing on its own about this week's raw spend. The channel is on the diminishing-returns portion of the curve.
*Common wrong answer:* "H = 0.73 means we're at 73% efficiency, which is below the maximum." True, but the relevant threshold for the EC50 question is H = 0.5, not H = 1.0.

> **Also asked on the slides:** *"Channel EC₅₀ = 200, α = 2. Compute H(100), H(200), H(400)."* — Substituting into H(s) = sᵃ / (EC50ᵃ + sᵃ) with α = 2:
> H(100) = 100² / (200² + 100²) = 10,000 / 50,000 = **0.20**;
>
> H(200) = 200² / (200² + 200²) = **0.50** (the H(EC50) = 0.5 property, true for any EC50);
>
> H(400) = 400² / (200² + 400²) = 160,000 / 200,000 = **0.80**.
> Doubling spend from 100 to 200 buys +0.30 of effectiveness; doubling again from 200 to 400 buys +0.30 as well (0.50 → 0.80) — the same 0.30 for twice the incremental spend, so **half as much per dollar**. That is saturation, and it is why the answer to Q3 is "shift incrementally," not "shift everything."

**Q3.** Move budget from Digital (lower marginal ROI) to TV (higher). But **not the entire Digital budget at once**. As TV spend rises, TV's adstock rises, and — past its inflection — its Hill slope H′ falls, so TV's MR = β·H′/(1 − λ) falls with it; Digital's MR rises as its spend is cut. Shift a step at a time, recomputing both, until the MRs meet — and then check the predicted revenue, because equal MRs are necessary but not sufficient for the best split (Section 1.4C).
*Common wrong answer:* "Shift everything to TV since TV ROI is higher." Diminishing returns mean the last dollar moved to TV has a much lower marginal ROI than the first dollar.

> **Also asked on the slides:** *"Last-touch attribution shows Digital = 70% of conversions, TV = 10%. MMM shows TV = 35% contribution. Which is right — and why might they differ?"* — Neither is "wrong"; they measure different things, and for a budget decision the MMM figure is the relevant one. Last-touch gives 100% of the credit to whatever the customer clicked last, so it systematically over-credits demand-harvesting channels and under-credits demand-creating ones: as Section 1.2 puts it: "Paid search captures demand. It does not create it." A customer may have learned about the brand from a TV spot, seen social posts, then clicked a digital ad — last touch records only the click. MMM models all channels simultaneously with their own adstock and saturation, so TV's carryover (high λ) shows up as contribution that last touch cannot see. The practical warning in Section 1.2 is exactly this case: shifting budget from TV to digital on last-touch evidence looks successful short-term while you are harvesting awareness TV built.

**Q4.** **False.** λ = 0 means instant decay — each period's advertising effect disappears completely before the next period. The channel can still have immediate in-period impact (S_t contributes fully to A_t). λ = 0 means no carryover, not no effect.
*Common wrong answer:* True. Confusing "no carryover" with "no effect."

**Q5.** TV campaigns should be **spaced further apart** than paid search campaigns. High λ = slow decay = the effects of one TV campaign persist for weeks. You don't need to run TV every week to maintain impact. Paid search (λ = 0.19) decays quickly — its effects are mostly exhausted within 1–2 weeks, so continuous presence is needed.
*Common wrong answer:* "TV should run continuously since it has a higher λ." High λ means the campaign persists longer, so you can run *less frequently* and still maintain cumulative impact.

### Section 2.1 — Worked Example
#### (~30 minutes | Hybrid: attempt Parts A–B first, then reveal)


---

**Problem Statement**

Weekly TV spend ($000s): $S_1 = 100$, $S_2 = 0$, $S_3 = 150$, $S_4 = 80$. Decay: $\lambda = 0.6$. Hill parameters: EC50 = 120, $\alpha = 2$.

**Part A:** Compute $A_1, A_2, A_3, A_4$.

**Part B:** Compute $H(A_3)$ and $H(A_4)$.

**Part C:** Repeat Part A with $\lambda_D = 0.2$ (Digital channel, same spend). Compare adstock values and explain the difference.

---

**Full Solution**

**Part A — TV Adstock ($\lambda = 0.6$)**

$$A_1 = 100 + 0.6 \times 0 = \mathbf{100.0}$$
$$A_2 = 0 + 0.6 \times 100 = \mathbf{60.0}$$
$$A_3 = 150 + 0.6 \times 60 = 150 + 36 = \mathbf{186.0}$$
$$A_4 = 80 + 0.6 \times 186 = 80 + 111.6 = \mathbf{191.6}$$

Even in week 2 (no spend), adstock = 60 — the memory of the prior campaign. By week 3, adstock reaches 186 though only \$150k was spent, because it carries forward week-1 and week-2 memory.

**Part B — Hill Responses**

At $A_3 = 186$, EC50 = 120, $\alpha = 2$:

$$H(186) = \frac{186^2}{120^2 + 186^2} = \frac{34{,}596}{14{,}400 + 34{,}596} = \frac{34{,}596}{48{,}996} \approx \mathbf{0.706}$$

At $A_4 = 191.6$:

$$H(191.6) = \frac{191.6^2}{120^2 + 191.6^2} = \frac{36{,}711}{14{,}400 + 36{,}711} = \frac{36{,}711}{51{,}111} \approx \mathbf{0.718}$$

Both exceed 0.5, meaning adstock is above EC50 in both weeks — TV is operating on the flattening (diminishing-returns) portion of the curve.

**Sanity check:** $H(120) = 120^2/(120^2 + 120^2) = 1/2 = 0.5$ ✓

**Part C — Digital Adstock ($\lambda_D = 0.2$)**

$$A_1^D = 100, \quad A_2^D = 0 + 0.2 \times 100 = 20, \quad A_3^D = 150 + 0.2 \times 20 = 154, \quad A_4^D = 80 + 0.2 \times 154 = 110.8$$

| Week | TV ($\lambda=0.6$) | Digital ($\lambda=0.2$) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 100.0 | 100.0 |
| 2 | 60.0 | 20.0 |
| 3 | 186.0 | 154.0 |
| 4 | 191.6 | 110.8 |

Digital drops far faster in week 2 (100 → 20 vs. 100 → 60 for TV). TV accumulates more memory, producing higher week-4 adstock despite identical raw spending.

### Section 2.2 — Interpretation Guide
#### (~10 minutes)

**Decay parameters (λ per channel):** Plausible ranges:
- TV, radio: 0.6–0.9
- Digital display, paid social: 0.3–0.6
- Paid search: 0.1–0.3

If paid search shows $\lambda = 0.85$, investigate — this likely reflects a data or model problem.

**EC50 values:** Compare to the actual **adstock** range in your data — this is Section 1.4B's convention applied to a fitted model, not a new rule: adstock, not raw spend, is what the Hill function takes as input, and adstock is always the larger of the two. If EC50 is far above your maximum adstock, you are far from saturation and still on the high-marginal-return part of the curve. If EC50 is below your minimum adstock, you are already past the half-saturation point. Comparing a fitted EC50 against the raw spend column instead is the single most common misreading of an MMM output, and it biases every conclusion the same way — toward thinking you have more **headroom** — room to add spend before the curve flattens — than you do.

**β coefficients:** The maximum revenue attributable to each channel. A channel with β = \$500k can contribute at most \$500k in weekly revenue (when its Hill response approaches 1). **β₀ is not a channel coefficient** — it is the intercept, baseline revenue with no advertising response at all, and reading it as a channel's plateau will overstate what advertising is doing.

**Marginal ROI:** Not something the fit prints — it is computed *from* the fit, one number per channel: MR_j = β_j × H′(A_j) / (1 − λ_j) at that channel's current adstock (Section 1.4C — and the
1/(1 − λ_j) is the step most write-ups drop). Read it as "dollars of revenue per extra dollar of spend", which is what makes it comparable across channels in a way that λ, EC50, β and H individually are not. A big-β channel with a small Hill slope can be worth less at the margin than a small-β channel still early on its curve, so **check that the recommendation ranks channels on MR — not on which channel has the biggest β, the lowest EC50, or the lowest H.** If the agent reports a reallocation without reporting marginal ROIs, it has not shown you its reason.

**Budget recommendation:** Always check: is the recommended reallocation extreme? A recommendation to shift 80% of budget to one channel should trigger scrutiny. Verify that the predicted revenue improvement is meaningful and that the model fit is good before acting on it.

### Common Misconceptions

**1. "Adstock replaces raw spend as the input — the model ignores current spend."**
No. Adstock includes current spend plus a weighted sum of all past spend. Current spend always enters adstock with full weight ($\lambda^0 = 1$). Adstock is an enrichment of the raw spend variable, not a replacement.

**2. "A higher λ always means the channel is more effective."**
λ is a decay rate, not an effectiveness measure. TV might have high λ (slow decay) and still generate less revenue than paid search with low λ (fast decay) if paid search has higher β or lower EC50.

**3. "The Hill function directly gives the revenue contribution of a channel."**
No. The Hill function returns a number between 0 and 1. To get revenue, multiply by β. The Hill function only tells you what proportion of the channel's maximum contribution is being achieved at the current adstock level.

**4. "Budget optimization tells us how much to spend in total."**
Budget optimization tells you how to allocate a fixed total budget — it does not tell you whether to spend more or less overall. That needs a different rule — keep adding spend while MR exceeds 1/margin (the footnote in Section 1.4C).

**5. "If marginal ROI is equal across channels, the model is wrong."**
Equal marginal ROI across the funded channels is what the best interior allocation looks like, so seeing it is not a warning sign. But it is necessary, not sufficient (Section 1.4C): with S-shaped curves a split can have equal MRs and still be among the worst, and the best allocation can be a corner that drops a channel. Judge an allocation by its predicted revenue, not by the equality alone.

### From Theory to Agent

| Agent step | Corresponds to |
|---|---|
| Computes $A_t = S_t + \lambda A_{t-1}$ | Section 1.4A — adstock recursion |
| Applies $H(A; \text{EC50}, \alpha)$ | Section 1.4B — Hill function |
| Fits regression on transformed variables | Lecture 2 — OLS on $\beta_c \cdot H(A_{c,t})$ |
| Reports fitted $\lambda$, EC50, $\alpha$ per channel | Sections 1.3, 1.4A, 1.4B — parameter interpretation ($\lambda$ in 1.4A) |
| Computes optimal budget allocation | Section 1.4C — highest predicted revenue at a fixed budget; funded channels end with equal marginal ROI |

**What to verify in the agent output:**
1. All λ values between 0 and 1
2. EC50 values in plausible range relative to the fitted **adstock** series — not relative to raw spend (Section 1.4B)
3. Predicted revenue at current spend ≈ actual average revenue (model fit sanity check)
4. Budget recommendation moves spending toward the channel with the higher **marginal ROI** — β × the Hill slope at its current adstock ÷ (1 − λ) (defined in Section 1.4C; the Hill slope itself is Section 1.1, Tool 3) — and not simply toward whichever channel sits lower relative to its own EC50. A large β can outweigh being further up the saturation curve.
